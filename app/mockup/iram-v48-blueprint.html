IRAM v48 Restructure

Proposal · v48 · for review before any code is written

Restructuring the terminal around one source of truth

IRAM has sixteen desks, seven menus and no settings screen. Preferences are scattered across six surfaces, and two of them disagree about how much money you have. The bottom bar reports on the terminal rather than on the trade, and the card that once said “here is the setup, here is the size” did not survive the rewrite. This is the plan to fix the structure and put those three things back — without discarding the chrome work that v45–v47 already measured and paid for — and to make the whole thing quicker under the hand: right-click that works, tables you can sort and select in, one field that finds anything, and undo that covers more than drawings.

Part I

What is wrong

One measured defect and one structural problem. Everything after this is a response to these two.

01The defect

Your equity is stored twice, and the two copies drift

Before any redesign argument, one measured fact. It is the reason this proposal leads with settings rather than with visuals.

The Risk desk writes account configuration to the store slot risk.config. The Calculator writes its own to calculator.v1. Both define equity and riskPct. Both default to 10,000 and 1%. Neither reads the other.

What that means at the desk

Set your equity to £4,200 on the Risk desk. Open the Calculator. It sizes your next position off £10,000 — and says nothing, because as far as it knows that is your equity. The lot size it returns is 2.4× too large, and it looks exactly as authoritative as a correct one.

This is the same failure class as the v47 pip-value and margin findings: not a wrong pixel, a confident wrong number.

A settings screen is normally a convenience feature. Here it is the structural fix — the place where account state stops being a thing each desk keeps a private copy of.

Why the Calculator folds into Risk

They are the same question asked twice. Risk answers "how big, given my stop"; the Calculator answers "what is this trade worth, given that size". They read the same account, the same instrument spec and the same pip value — which is precisely why they were able to disagree. As tabs of one desk they cannot.

Why Settings is not a desk

Every desk is a view of the market. Settings is a view of the terminal. Putting it on the desk bar would say the opposite, and would cost a slot on a row that already measured as full at 1200px.

Part II

How it feels to use

The first part is about what is wrong and how the terminal is arranged. This part is about the thing you actually asked for: making it powerful, organised and quick under the hand. Six phases, and a rule that governs all of them.

What “smart” is allowed to mean here

Smart is the terminal doing the fetching, the watching and the arithmetic. It is not the terminal doing the concluding. Everything below removes work — a desk switch, a retype, a manual sort, a lost pane, a number you had to go and find. None of it adds a claim.

That line is not a style preference. With PBO at 89%, a terminal that got “smarter” by asserting more would be getting worse. Power here means fewer keystrokes between you and a fact, never more confidence in a guess.

03Act in place

Right-click does nothing, almost everywhere

I counted. Across twenty-seven UI modules there is exactly one oncontextmenu handler, on a workspace pane. Right-clicking the chart, a watchlist row, a drawing, a detected zone or an alert does nothing at all.

That single fact explains most of why a fifteen-desk terminal feels like navigation rather than work. Every action you can take on a thing currently lives somewhere that is not that thing — a menu at the top of the window, or a desk you have to switch to and switch back from. You go to the tool; the tool never comes to you.

Two changes, and the second matters more

Right-click menus on every object, built from the command registry that already exists, so each menu is generated rather than hand-listed and can never drift from the commands it names. On the chart: alert at this price, measure from here, draw from here, set a stop here, copy the level. On a row: open it, send it to a pane, compare, alert, remove. On a drawing: edit, duplicate, lock, colour, delete. On a detection: explain it, project it to a higher timeframe, alert me when it is retested.

The dock becomes contextual. Today it is a fixed inspector. Make it follow selection: with nothing selected it holds the Setup card and the read; select a drawing and it becomes that drawing's properties; select a detected zone and it becomes that zone's evidence and provenance — without leaving the chart. That is the mechanism that turns fifteen desks from fifteen places you must go into fifteen things that can come to you.

04Tables

A screener you cannot sort is a list

Measured across the whole of src/ui: zero occurrences of any column-sort machinery — no sortKey, no onSort, no sort direction state anywhere. And no selection set in the screener, the watchlist or the alerts desk, which means no multi-select exists in the terminal at all.

The screener scans a universe and renders it in the order the scan happened to finish. You cannot rank by score, filter to one class of instrument, select six rows and alert on all of them, or keep the arrangement you made yesterday. Every table in the terminal has this same shape, because every table was written separately.

One table primitive, used by five desks

Screener, watchlist, alerts, journal and the data archive all get the same component, so a habit learned on one works on the rest.

05Find · undo

Find anything by typing it. Undo anything you did.

Two changes that between them decide whether the terminal feels fast and safe, and both are mostly already built.

The palette becomes an omnibox

The palette already has a prefix-source architecture — four sources are registered today: commands under >, symbols, raw symbols, and detected structures under @. The hard part exists. What it cannot find is a setting, an alert, a saved workspace, a journal entry, a help topic or a desk, which is why you still hunt through menus for things you know the name of.

Add those as sources and one field answers “where is that thing” for the whole application. Typing with no prefix searches everything and ranks by what you use.

Undo covers two commands out of sixty-two

Measured: draw.undo and draw.redo are the only undo commands in the terminal. Close a pane, clear a watchlist, delete an alert, reset a settings section — none of it comes back.

The workspace model already makes this nearly free. Its own header says a pure tree is “what makes undo one line” — and then nothing calls it. So: one application-level stack. Every destructive action pushes an entry carrying a sentence describing itself, the toast that confirms it also offers to reverse it, and Ctrl Z walks back through the lot.

And the thing presets cannot do

Workspace presets exist; a workspace you built yourself cannot be named, saved or recalled. Four panes arranged the way you like, on the symbols you were watching, is currently something you rebuild by hand every morning. Named workspaces make it a keystroke — and the model already serialises to JSON, so this is storage, not architecture.

06Command bar

Seven menus become one, and the palette stops hiding behind an icon

The top row carries a brand, seven text menus, five desk groups and five icon buttons — seventeen targets on forty-two pixels. The deskbar module's own comment records that this row measured as full at 1200px.

The menus are the least-earning thing on it. Every command inside them is already in the palette — the source says so explicitly — and View ▸ Desks duplicates the desk bar sitting two centimetres to its right. Meanwhile the palette itself, which is the fastest route to all sixty-two commands, is a 27-pixel magnifier that gives no hint it exists.

Modern chrome is not fewer affordances. It is a ranked row: where you are, then the thing you type into, then what is true right now.

07Live bar

One row that answers “where am I, and what am I in?”

The status bar today is plumbing: feed source, latency, bar count, stream state, a note and the clock. All useful, none of it about the market and none of it about your money.

The v39 build had a live ticker carrying prices, day range, candle countdown, news countdown, regime and ADX, ATR, RSI, funding, open P&L, today's P&L, heat and the guard state. None of it survived into the rewrite. What replaced it reports on the terminal rather than on the trade.

Rather than add a fourth chrome row — v45 and v46 were spent removing rows — the status bar becomes the live bar. The plumbing collapses into one health chip that expands when clicked, and the space it frees carries the numbers.

Why it does not scroll

The v39 bar was a marquee cycling every field in turn. You cannot find a number that is moving, and you certainly cannot compare two. Fixed zones in a fixed order mean your eye learns one map and keeps it. Values update in place; the layout does not.

The rule that governs every field

A field with no source is omitted, never zeroed and never carried forward. The dashed slot above is what “not covered” looks like — on-chain data does not exist for this instrument, so the bar says so rather than printing a hyphen that could be misread as zero. A value that is covered but late renders dimmed with its age beside it. Those three states look different because the response to them is different.

Clicking the health chip expands today's status bar in full — source, latency, bars, stream state, reconnects, the note. Nothing was deleted; it stopped occupying the row by default.

Measured while drawing this. My first field list came to 1,377px of content in a 1,280px bar — RSI, the funding countdown, the day-range percentage, the position count and the UTC clock all had to go before it fitted, and RSI is already on the chart behind it. So the bar needs the same discipline the desk switcher already has: it measures its own children against its own width and drops the lowest-ranked field first, rather than clipping, wrapping, or shrinking the type. The rank is fixed and visible in Settings, so what you lose at 1,100px is a decision rather than an accident.

08Setup

The card that says whether there is a trade, and exactly what it is

v39 had this and called it the Sniper Card. I searched the whole of app/src: there is no match for it anywhere in the rewrite.

The Decision desk that stands in its place shows a weighted read and its evidence, which answers what is happening. It never answers so what do I do, at what size, and what would stop me. That gap is the reason a fourteen-desk terminal can still leave you doing arithmetic on paper at the moment of decision.

Everything it needs already exists — sizePosition, stopFromAtr, rewardToRisk, checkGuards and portfolioHeat in risk/sizing.ts, detectStructure for the swing that places the stop, the live spread, the calendar and the decision read. This is composition, not new analysis, which is why it is a small build rather than a large one.

Say this part out loud before building it

A recommended setup is the most dangerous screen in this terminal. Your standing note on this project is not to automate anything off the confluence score — PBO 89%, meaning the best in-sample configuration lands below the median out of sample roughly nine times in ten. A card that prints an entry, a stop and a lot size is precisely the surface that invites someone to stop reading everything else.

So the guards are structural, not cosmetic: the PBO figure and this setup type's real record sit on the card permanently, at the same weight as the numbers — never in a tooltip. The verdict language is about your rules and never about the future. And STAND DOWN is not a view on direction — the card says so in words, because a trader who reads a blocked setup as “bearish” has been misled by the thing meant to protect them.

If you would rather this did not exist at all, say so and I will drop it. I would rather build it with these guards than leave the question unanswered on paper.

Fixed read order — the same six blocks, every time

The order is the design. A card whose layout changes with the verdict teaches you to hunt for the good news.

Why the plan and the size disappear

A blocked card shows no entry, no stop and no lot size. Leaving them on screen greyed out is an invitation to retype them by hand, which is the exact operational error the gate exists to prevent.

Why both failures are named

Not “blocked” — which gate, by how much, and what clears it. A gate you cannot argue with is a gate you learn to disable.

Why the read is repeated here

Score 78 and coverage 86% are printed on the blocked card too. Without them, STAND DOWN reads as “the market is bearish” — the card would be quietly manufacturing a directional opinion it does not hold.

Where it lives

Top of the right dock on the Chart desk, so it never costs a desk switch at the moment of decision, and in full on the Decision desk beside the evidence that produced it.

Part III

What it is missing

Screens a professional tool is expected to have and this one does not: a settings surface, feed provenance, an editable keymap, a trade journal and a first run that does not open on an empty form.

12Journal

The desk that closes the loop

The terminal produces a confluence score, a decision verdict, a size and a checklist — and then never finds out whether any of it was right. Every other desk points forward. Nothing points back.

The Journal records a decision at the moment it is made, together with the evidence that was on screen at the time: the verdict, the score and its coverage, which sources were live, the spread you actually paid. When the trade closes, the outcome is attached. Over a hundred entries that becomes the only honest answer to "does this terminal help me?" — measured on your trades rather than asserted in a backtest.

Why this belongs here and not in a spreadsheet

A spreadsheet records what you remember about a trade. The Journal records what the terminal was showing when you took it — including that two of six sources were down, or that the score was 71 with only 40% coverage. That is the part you cannot reconstruct afterwards, and it is the part that explains the result.

The migration that has to be right

Two existing slots collapse into one. If a user has set equity on the Risk desk and never touched the Calculator, or the reverse, one of the two values is real and the other is a default. Silently picking either is a coin flip on the number every position size depends on.

Rule: if the two disagree and neither is the default, migration keeps neither. It writes the risk.config value, marks the setting unconfirmed, and the first thing Settings shows is both numbers and the question of which is yours. One dialog, once — that is cheaper than a wrong lot size.

What I am deliberately not proposing

A new visual language. The token layer, the five themes, the three densities and the merged command bar are the product of measurements written down in the source — the 77% of type on two size steps, the eleven desks that fell into a “More” menu at 1200px, the font stack that resolved to plain Segoe UI on this machine. Replacing that with a fresh look would throw away findings and buy nothing. Everything above reuses the existing tokens exactly — all nine mockups are built from tokens.css, not from a new palette.

Steps 2 to 6 are where I am proposing to change how the terminal is operated, and none of it is a restyle. Two of those changes are subtractive — seven menus become one, and a row of plumbing becomes a row of numbers. The other three add capability the terminal simply does not have: a right-click handler where there is currently one in twenty-seven modules, column sorting where there is currently none, and an undo stack that reaches past the two drawing commands. No new colour, no new type size, no row grows taller, and nothing reachable today stops being reachable.

Nothing has been built. This is a proposal against the current tree at C:\Users\syedm\Downloads\Projects. The one measured claim in it — the duplicated equity slot — is verifiable at app/src/ui/risk.ts:68 and app/src/ui/calculator.ts:78.

Fifteen phases is a lot to approve at once, so treat Part II as the answer to “make it feel professional” and Part III as the answer to “it is missing things”. They are independent — you can take either first.
